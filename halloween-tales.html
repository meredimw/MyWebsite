2026 Entry

And the Devil Leapt onto his Tongue

Orson II • Halloween Writing Contest • Literary / Psychological Horror
Biblical Horror Prophecy Psychological Horror Language & Belief

In June of 2026, after a free outdoor festival, a man gave me a Bible.

English is a coward’s language because it permits a sentence to conceal its causes. A man gave me a Bible. The rain fell. Caesar crossed the Rubicon. Abel died. Christ rose.

Things simply happen in English.

Who sent the man? Who put the Bible in his hand? Who put me in the park? Grammar has no answer. Grammar is an executioner’s mask for God.

But this man came to me. “Would you like a Bible?” That was all. I said yes. I do not remember his face. You will say this is convenient. That is fine. I’ll not remember yours either.

I carried the Bible home.

I had seen prophecy before. But that summer the coincidences had become unusually frequent. A billboard would answer a question I had been considering ten minutes earlier. A stranger would say a word I had written that morning. I would enter a bookstore, take a novel from a shelf without reading its title, open it, put my finger upon a sentence, and find there, not always, but often enough, something resembling an answer.

Not an answer like: YES. Or: NO. The answers were crooked things. Symbols. Names. Fragments. A river. A door. A father. A knife.

Prophecy is repetition. So I decided to perform an experiment. Experiment. That is an excellent modern word. Put a white coat upon superstition and suddenly everyone respects it.

I bought a notebook. On the first page I wrote the date. Then I closed my eyes. I held the Bible by its spine and let the pages run beneath my thumb. Frrrrrrrrrrrrrrp. A ridiculous little sound.

I stopped.

I placed my finger upon the page.

Then I opened my eyes.

The chapter number was the month. The verse number was the day.

I stared at it.

Then I laughed.

Of course I laughed. What else should a reasonable man do when the universe first introduces itself?

I wrote the result in my notebook.

Three days later I tried again.

Date first. Bible closed. Eyes closed. Pages. Finger. Open. Month. Day. Again.

Now twice is nothing. Any gambler knows that. Three times is interesting. Four times is irritating. Five times requires arithmetic. Six times requires theology. By the tenth time, you have either discovered something about the universe or the universe has discovered something about you.

Ten. Ten exact correspondences. Not ten approximately. Not nearly ten. Ten exactly. Month. Day. Month. Day. Month. Day.

I calculated the odds.

Then I calculated them again.

Numbers are another coward’s language. We invented them because words lied too obviously. But numbers lie with straighter faces. Still, every calculation told me the same thing. Impossible.

I considered fraud.

Perhaps the Bible had been altered. But how? The man could not know where my finger would fall. Perhaps someone entered my room while I slept and replaced it. Again: the finger. Perhaps I unconsciously selected the correct pages. But I did not know where they were. Perhaps. Perhaps. Perhaps. That is the atheist’s rosary. Eventually a man must stop saying perhaps.

So I accepted the conclusion demanded by the evidence. The Bible was speaking to me. It should have comforted me. It did not. Because I had been so occupied with the numbers that I had neglected the words.

I went back to the notebook. You must understand that the Bible is not the book they show children. Children get boats. Children get rainbows. Children get animals walking two by two beneath a smiling yellow sun.

They do not get the bodies afterward. They do not get the ruined cities. They do not get the dead mothers. They do not get the prophets opening kings like sacks of grain.

The Bible has teeth. But we file them down for Sunday school. My finger found every tooth. I began to dread the readings. Naturally, I continued them. This is another thing you misunderstand about fear. Fear does not make us flee. Not always.

The seventh reading took me to Abraham. You know the story. Of course you know the story. A father. A son. A mountain. A knife. And God, who wishes to know whether He is loved.

There is still a boy tied upon the wood.

There is still a knife.

I closed the Bible.

For the first time, I did not record the verse.

That night I dreamed of the park. The festival was gone. The tents were gone. The people were gone. Only the man remained. I still could not see his face. He held out the Bible. But when I reached for it he said: “Note the book.”

I woke before dawn.

The next morning I wrote the missing verse into the notebook. Accuracy matters. The readings continued. Instead: Blood. Fire. Firstborn. Altar. Knife. Again and again the knife.

I began carrying one. Not because the Bible commanded it. Do not be childish. Books do not command anything. Ink cannot command. Paper cannot command. Words cannot command. That is precisely why they are so dangerous. They require someone else to finish the sentence. I carried the knife because carrying it made the passages quieter.

For a while.

Then came the animals. I will not describe that. You have imagination enough when it serves you. Afterward I told myself it was an experiment. The ancients sacrificed animals. Abraham sacrificed the ram. Leviticus prescribed blood because blood belonged to God. If prophecy were real, perhaps obedience would alter the sequence. That was my hypothesis. You see? Science.

I performed another reading. Month. Day. A passage concerning greater offerings.

I stopped sleeping.

I covered the mirrors.

I stopped answering the telephone.

The notebook filled.

Dates on the left. Verses on the right.

Between them I began drawing lines. The lines formed shapes. At first constellations. Then letters. Then something like a ladder. Or a tree. Or a gallows. All sacred symbols do.

By autumn I understood that I had made one fundamental error. I had believed the prophecy existed for my benefit. How American. How perfectly, obscenely American. We assume everything is addressed to us because everything arrives with our name printed upon the package. But the prophets were not given futures so they could improve their circumstances. Jonah did not receive investment advice. Jeremiah was not told which woman to marry. Isaiah was not given lottery numbers. Prophecy is not information. It is conscription.

Once you know, you belong to what you know.

I returned to the park. The leaves had begun to fall. There was no festival. No tents. No music. No man. I sat upon the same bench with the Bible on my knees and the notebook beside me.

I had decided upon one final experiment.

One.

That is important.

I wrote the date. Slowly. Month. Day. I closed my eyes. The pages moved beneath my thumb.

Frrrrrrrrrrrrrrp.

Creation. Flood. Egypt. Kings. Prophets. Cross. Tomb.

Frrrrrrrrrrrrrrp.

I stopped.

My finger descended.

Before I opened my eyes, I knew.

Not the passage. Something worse. I knew that the number would match. And suddenly I understood that this was the true miracle. Not that I had guessed correctly. Not that God had guided my finger. But that for the first time in months I no longer possessed even the smallest hope of being wrong. I opened my eyes.

Month.

Day.

Of course.

The passage was Abraham again. But this time I read farther. People always stop too early. That is how religion survives. They stop with the angel. They stop with the ram. They stop with Abraham descending the mountain. They say: See? God did not require the sacrifice after all. And everyone goes home relieved. But prophecy does not stop because the reader is relieved. Blood had still been demanded. Something still died. The altar was not dismantled empty. I sat there until evening. Then I understood the mistake. All this time I had been searching for Isaac. Someone innocent. Someone beloved. Someone sufficiently valuable that surrendering him would prove the sincerity of the offering.

That was vanity.

There was only one thing I possessed completely. One thing whose surrender could not be counterfeit. The old theologians had a word for it. Self. No. Older than that. Soul. Better.

I went home.

I put the Bible on the table. Beside it, the notebook. Beside that, the knife. Three objects. Book. Record. Instrument. Past. Present. Future. Father. Son. Spirit. We cannot help making trinities. Give a man three stones and by nightfall he will have invented a religion.

I lit one candle. Not two. There would be no audience. That matters. A sacrifice performed for applause is theater. I read every entry in the notebook from beginning to end. June. July. August. September. October.

Month. Day. Month. Day. Month. Day. The numbers marched perfectly down the pages. Soldiers. Gravestones. Teeth.

I had wanted proof. There it was.

I had wanted prophecy. There it was.

I had wanted to know whether God could speak to a man. There are questions whose cruelty lies entirely in receiving an answer.

I opened the Bible one final time.

Not randomly.

There was no longer any need.

I opened to the place I had marked.

Abraham raised his hand. Isaac waited. Heaven watched.

And I laughed.

Because at last I understood the joke.

Abraham had an angel. Christ had resurrection. Jephthah had victory. Even Cain received a mark. Everyone gets something. That is how the stories persuade you. A bargain hidden inside obedience. But a true sacrifice cannot be a bargain.

A true sacrifice receives nothing. No ram. No angel. No resurrection. No voice saying “Stop!” Otherwise you have merely made a purchase. So I waited.

Five minutes. Ten.

The candle trembled.

A truck passed outside.

Somewhere in the building a toilet flushed.

This is what the universe sounds like when it declines to intervene.

And there it was. The final proof.

I looked at the notebook. Ten impossible dates. Ten impossible verses. Ten doors through which I had walked willingly. And now no eleventh door. Only me. Only the candle. Only the book. Only the instrument.

I will not tell you precisely what happened next. Precision belongs to experiments. This was no longer an experiment. I will tell you only that the candle eventually went out.

The notebook remained open.

In the morning, they found the notebook. They found the dates. They checked them. Some matched. Perhaps all of them. Perhaps none. That depends upon whose account you believe. There were explanations afterward. There are always explanations afterward.

Coincidence. Delusion. A damaged mind imposing patterns upon indifferent numbers. Perfectly respectable. But somewhere, even now, someone is sitting alone with a book.

Not necessarily a Bible. That would be too obvious. A dictionary. A telephone directory. A novel abandoned on a bus. They have a question. They open the book. They close their eyes. They place one finger upon one line.

And there it is: an answer.

They laugh. Of course they laugh.

The first time.

But somewhere before the tenth, the book stops answering the question they are asking. It begins asking one of its own. The only question prophecy has ever asked. Not: “Do you believe me?” The question is: “Will you obey?”

And if tonight you should happen to open some book at random, and your finger should fall upon something that seems intended particularly for you, close it.

Do not calculate anything.

Do not try again.

Do not write down the date.

And whatever you do—

do not ask for proof.

You might get it.

Happy Halloween!

2026 Critique Notes

Reviewer: Writers Programming (comments on the October 6, 2026 Word draft).

The notes below summarize the reviewer's comments in the document. The quotations are reproduced from those comments; the story above is the full submitted text, without rewriting.

What worked well

  • Prose and voice: The reviewer described the line-level writing as "absolutely fantastic" and "absolutely lovely," and singled out the story's strongest sentences.
  • Repetition and rhythm: The reviewer especially liked the narrator's escalating numerical pattern and the repetition that gives it momentum.
  • Early speculative hook: The reviewer praised the introduction of the uncanny Bible-date experiment early in the story.

Suggestions for revision

  • Clarify the central theme and character arc: The reviewer found the plot inconsistent and was unsure what the narrator learns, or what readers should take away. The recommendation was to identify the theme first and revise backward from it.
  • Reconcile competing ideas about language: The narrator describes English as cowardly, then calls words dangerous; the reviewer questioned the logic of that shift and other sweeping claims that seem at odds with the story itself.
  • Explain key actions: Why does the narrator cover the mirrors and stop answering the telephone? Why undertake another reading at that moment? The reviewer wanted clearer motivation and cause-and-effect.
  • Tighten the argument: Some passages felt preachy or rambling to the reviewer; a more focused through-line could make the powerful prose serve the story more directly.
  • Submission formatting: The reviewer recommended quarter-inch paragraph indents and double spacing for future submissions.

Overall assessment

“This piece has moments that truly shine! On a line level, your prose is absolutely lovely.”

The reviewer recommended more consistent plotting and a clearer controlling theme, while emphasizing the story's literary strengths. In the reviewer's words: “My suggestion for this is to find the theme you want to tell and work backwards from there.”

2025 Entry

In July

Bainbridge Island Halloween Writing Contest • Literary Horror / Weird Fiction
Folk Horror Weird Fiction Harvest Ritual Murden Fog

We know a remote farm on Bainbridge Island, where Ms. Buckley lives. In July, peas grow there.

What a ridiculous language.

Only in the New World can you be expected to begin a sentence with the word “in” and have it to bear weight at all. The elders had no such vanity. They began with Names, with things Eternal, things that struck the tongue like hammers on sacrificial altars. The Canaanites had a proper language. Aramaic — the tongue of Jesus — the only carriage strong enough to hold ideas that matter at all. But now? Now we say, with a sigh: Peas. Grow. There. In July.

They stand in planted rows, upright, against the rolling fog of Murden Cove — like confederate infantrymen with the rage of a thousand dead grandfathers. Mendel’s genetically perfect children, each pod the bastard edict of science, inheritance, and family. In July. Always in July. The word repeats with a slouch, without meaning. An annual metronome in the mouth of schoolmasters, politicians, news-readers.

Nihilists.

Subject, verb, predicate. Meaningless, idiotic, propaganda. The Germans had a proper language. Verbalnomen. They recognized there is no noun behind the verb. No do-er behind the deed. God - how different the history of human sociology, human morality, but for the damned grammar teachers. But, you see, the schools are gone now, the teachers are gone now, the peas are gone now. She has picked them. Mrs. Buckley’s hands, coarse from labor, took them from their stalks, neatly, carefully, and carried them off in their baskets – their bassinets and caskets. The peas are gone but their rows stand, empty, the fog hanging low. The Buckley house now waits, empty, except for me. And my work, undone.

This time of year, this sanctified time of year, she raises a crop of a different kind. Some might call them gourds, of this type or that: turnips, perhaps, or squash. But what is any word in this cursed tongue? Nothing – Shakespeare’s rose could have just have easily been a pumpkin. I call them pumpkins rather than flowers. Why? Because it pleases me and I make things what they are. And why not pumpkins? They are heavy enough. They are round enough. And when my blade enters, they resist just as a pumpkin ought to. And it is Halloween after all - so you’ll protest no more, you little pest, and realize that for thousands of years pumpkins are grown, and carved, on Halloween, on Samhein. A pumpkin is a pumpkin because I say it is, and that is the end of it.

If you’re still waiting for a perfect orange caricature, bought from a corporate town and borne of alchemy, with a vine and price still clinging to it, you are an imbecile, a grammarian, a petty professor — the sort who would scrape barnacles from the hull of language and polish them up for sale, abandoning the ship for the shells, thinking it is the husk makes the fruit. I should like to show you the meaning of an honest day’s work —what it takes to grow a pumpkin of this sort.

So, stop your quibbling and watch as I carve. Here, now, there is no poetry, no metaphor, no language - only my knife.

It goes in — and the flesh parts. The sound is polite. Small and wet. The smell: thick, sugary, full of copper, you know it well. I reach in with my hand. Strings cling to it. Seeds stick to it — slick, red, glistening. They come away in my fist and fall with a splatter on the wooden floor-boards. You, save the seeds. We must save the seeds. Tradition demands it, and we keep tradition. If you read your Genesis, you’ll read of seeds like this. If you read Leviticus, you’ll read of blood like this. The Fallen scatter both. And the Lord harvests from each of them, just the same.

Collect them: I have carving to do; creating to do—the eyes first, always the eyes. The windows to the Soul. The Fallen, we make our own souls, now. Deep triangular souls: black shadows, staring at nothing – like everyone honest with themselves does. I scrape them clean. Now, the mouth – no room for the body. The mouth fights – like every other mouth living today. It grins, a cave - full of teeth. Stubborn teeth, but I will have them. I force them, one by one, until they sit in an obedient row, like the stones of Puma Punka that only a being of the sky could see properly; could only make properly. Count them if you must. I do not. What is the number to me – another illusion, another rose, another pumpkin.

Now watch as I place the candle – Plato’s light; it does nothing but show us shadows, but that is all we have ever really seen – except this: we have made a face free of shadows – we make what light cannot. It glows. Red. It looks at me, and I look back. And we know each other. Now tell me, my Pumpkin: who is the abyss, and who, the monster. Stingy Jack: tried to trick the Devil but was instead denied by God. Now he has only this coal for light. A lantern in the dark that is not enough to see in this Murden Fog, at least not enough to see what really matters.

But our work is not done. No work, by anyone, is done until the Earth takes them. Look no further than the peas. The peas are gone, but the ground cries for them to replenish it. The soil remembers the peas. Mrs. Buckley’s hands took them, but mine will return to it something stronger, better. Her shovel waits for me. I open the pit. The dark Island soil is hungry for what has been taken from it, what has been stolen from it. History is always a history of theft; but this Halloween, something is finally given back.

I lower the lantern, cradled the way a mother might her babe, someone’s babe. Then the soil. Again and again the soil. Only soil. Not a mound. Not a trace. Only the soil, tamped down, covered by the lingering fog. The empty pea stalks, rattling in the night air - like bones.

And when July comes, peas will grow once again on Bainbridge Island. Mrs. Buckley will pick them, and she will smile at how sweet they are. And you will taste them and say, “How fine the peas grow where Mrs. Buckley lives, on Bainbridge Island. In July.”

But you will not know — not ever — what lantern burns beneath your feet.

Only I will know.

Happy Halloween.

2025 Critique Notes

Emily Smiley, BARN Writers’ Studio Programming Coordinator:

Things that worked well

  • The prose was described as literary, poetic, and “absolutely fantastic.”
  • The pumpkin carving sequence was singled out as the strongest section.
  • The sensory details were especially vivid: the feel of the pumpkin seeds, the sound of the innards, and the smell of fresh pumpkin.

Suggestions for revision

  • Cut back on a few metaphors so the strongest ones land harder.
  • Clarify or trim some of the more mysterious language where it creates confusion rather than atmosphere.
  • Lean even more into concrete sensory details, especially around the peas and farm setting.

Overall, the critique emphasized that the story’s prose and atmosphere were strong, and that a more focused revision could make the horror sharper and more immediate.
